Saurabh

Postdoctoral Research Associate · AI3, BASP lab, Heriot-Watt University

I am a Postdoctoral Research Associate in AI3, a new group within the BASP lab at Heriot-Watt University in Edinburgh. I am part of the TomoGrav project, which is developing dynamic gravitational tomography: a way of combining radio observations of black holes with AI-driven imaging to reconstruct how the hot plasma around M87* and Sagittarius A* moves in three dimensions over time. The goal is to produce the first 3D movies of black holes (group website). I am also a member of the Event Horizon Telescope Collaboration, and I completed my PhD at the Max Planck Institute for Radio Astronomy in Bonn.

My work is about seeing black holes. I use very long baseline interferometry (VLBI), which links radio telescopes across the Earth into one planet-sized telescope. I reconstruct images from its sparse data. I predict what those images should look like, by tracing light through curved spacetime and simulating the hot gas with general-relativistic magnetohydrodynamics (GRMHD). And I ask what the images reveal about the giant black holes at the centres of galaxies, like the one at the centre of our own.

Where the jet of M87* begins

The 2021 EHT observations hold more than the bright ring. We found a faint, compact component about 0.09 light-years from the black hole, along the jet direction: a candidate for the jet's launching point.

Read the paper ↗

Accretion flows and magnetic fields

How do the shape of the disk, the motion of the plasma and the geometry of the magnetic field set the ring we see? We compare polarized model images against EHT measurements to narrow down which configurations M87* allows.

Read the paper ↗

Is M87* really a black hole?

General relativity does not demand that collapse ends in an event horizon. It also allows horizonless objects, with no point of no return at all. I ran the first 3D GRMHD simulation of gas accreting onto one, the JMN-1 spacetime. At today's EHT resolution its image is almost indistinguishable from a black hole's. The difference hides inside the shadow, where the next generation of telescopes could look.

Read the preprint ↗

Simulations

How do you simulate matter falling onto a black hole, and what a telescope would see of it? I build up from simple analytic disks to full GRMHD, ray tracing each model through curved spacetime. The black hole behind this page is traced live in your browser.

Science outreach

  • Stellar Universe

    In 2017, with fellow students at Dyal Singh College, University of Delhi, I founded Stellar Universe to bring astrophysics and general relativity to students from the undergraduate level on, whatever their background. It grew from a group of friends into a network of more than 500 science enthusiasts in India and abroad, running free lectures, courses in general relativity, programming workshops and sky-watching sessions, with support from the Nehru Planetarium, Delhi.

Out of the Box

It may seem that my world revolves around black holes. In a sense, it does. I spend much of my time either lost in thought or lost in the experience of living, though I have never been particularly good at separating the two. Curiously, my thoughts often lead me beyond the very science that inspires them. What does it mean to observe something whose defining boundary permits no light to return? If two fundamentally different spacetimes cast the same shadow, have we discovered the truth, or merely found an explanation that fits? Where does observation end and interpretation begin? That is where I find myself dancing on the thin line between science and philosophy, and, on braver days, dangling off the edge. Perhaps that is what draws me to the philosophy of science: the suspicion that understanding the universe and understanding what we can know about it are not quite the same endeavour.

But life cannot be lived entirely in thought (or maybe it can?), and not everything worth expressing lends itself to an equation.

Somewhere in the final months of my PhD, amid the rather complicated relationship with my thesis writing, I taught myself to play the bansuri, the Indian bamboo flute. It began as a modest escape from stress and gradually became something of a refuge. I make no claims to understanding the intricacies of classical music; I know little of its grammar, only something of its language. My encounters with the ghazal and the qawwali have turned music from something I hear into something I feel. Put simply: the ghazal is a medicine for the pain and misery of life; the qawwali is the ecstasy that carries one beyond them.

Whatever spills out of all this lands here: essays, poetry, photographs and more.
