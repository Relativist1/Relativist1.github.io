On the questions I pursue

As you may have already guessed, I have a special affinity for black holes. While that is true, it’s not just that.

What drew me to these objects was simply this: the weird things happening in space which are not intuitive. Let me put it this way: the implications of Einstein’s theory are so bizarre that they make it much more interesting than what Newton came up with. This is in no way a reason to look down on the said gentleman, but I think we should explore this a bit more. There had not been much advancement in non-Euclidean geometry, and modern mathematics was still a bit far away. He did the best that could be done, and maybe even more. Given the status of the said person, his theory went on to be unchallenged for more than 200 years (before you attack me, it is implicit that the theory was well tested against observations, which was the major reason). Einstein then stood on his shoulders (after standing on someone else’s feet, knees, back, and ultimately making his way to the shoulders) and then dropped an apple.

If you are still wondering what the point of all this was, perhaps nothing? But I am pessimistically optimistic, so there could definitely be something. From my understanding: the path you take, and the line of reasoning you choose, will land you on someone else’s shoulders or maybe somewhere else, I can’t say for certain. But at least a step forward in finally accepting this: If reality is what it seems to us, then why even bother doing the elaborate calculations and draining useless water in some data center which could have been used to water my plants, which are on the verge of dying on my study desk? But if there’s an objective reality, what is the best way for us to perceive it in a way that is comprehensible to us through the traditional means of the senses we have (in simple words, can I become the Buddha, if Buddha really achieved what I just mentioned?). This line of reasoning can be circular if I want it to be, but I will not. I will assume that there’s an objective reality out there (yes, pessimistic optimism and a pinch of hypocrisy according to the philosophers), then what’s the best we can say about it?

Now, it would be rather convenient if all I wanted to know was whether Einstein was right (or wrong, depending on which side you are betting on). But that would be making the whole affair unnecessarily simple, which physics, unfortunately, has rarely allowed us to do. What interests me more is the rather peculiar business of taking some measurements, putting them through an assortment of equations, simulations, and assumptions, and finally arriving at a statement about what the universe is doing. There is gravity involved, certainly, but also matter, magnetic fields, and occasionally our own enthusiasm to believe that we have understood something. I suppose the question is how much of our explanation actually belongs to nature, and how much we have rather generously contributed ourselves. This is not to suggest that we know nothing (before someone decides to revoke my degree), but perhaps we ought to be a little more careful about what exactly we claim to know. I find this a sufficiently interesting problem to spend a considerable portion of my life on, although whether that is a wise decision is an entirely different question.

This could be the closest I can get to explaining the questions I would take up in my academic research as a physicist. Normally, physicists can be painstakingly annoying, as sometimes their explanations can be really dear to them. I would, with pleasure, cross them to see how annoying I can be.

The papers that I am working on, or will work on, will simply be a reflection of this.
